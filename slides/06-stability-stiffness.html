Think first

The exact solution of the decay equation decays. How can the numerical solution grow?

  • Forward Euler with \(k \Delta t > 2\) produces a solution that grows without bound
  • Write down \(u_{n+1}\) in terms of \(u_n\)
  • Ask: what does the method do to a small error that is already present in \(u_n\)?

The test equation

Small perturbations of a solution

Goal: stability for a general system is decided by the scalar test equation \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = \mu u\), with \(\mu\) the eigenvalues of the Jacobian, and these can be complex.

  • Stability is about errors: an error made in one step (truncation, round-off) is carried along by all later steps
  • \(u(t)\): a solution
  • \(u(t) + \delta(t)\): a second solution close to it, \(\delta(t)\): the perturbation
  • Both satisfy the ODE:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(t, u), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}(u + \delta)}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(t, u + \delta) \]

Subtracting gives an exact equation for the perturbation:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\delta}{\mathrm{d}t} = f(t, u + \delta) - f(t, u) \]

Linearizing the perturbation equation

  • Assumption: \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable in \(u\)
  • Taylor series: \(f(t, u + \delta) = f(t, u) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(t, u)\,\delta + O(\delta^2)\)
  • Inserted, \(f(t, u)\) cancels:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\delta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\big(t, u(t)\big) \, \delta + O(\delta^2) \]

The linearized equation and the test equation

  • For a small perturbation, \(O(\delta^2)\) is much smaller than the linear term
  • Neglecting it gives the linearized equation:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\delta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\big(t, u(t)\big) \, \delta \tag{6.1} \]

Freezing the coefficient: the test equation

  • The derivative changes with time along \(u(t)\)
  • Freezing the coefficient: treat it as a constant \(\mu\) over a few time steps
  • An assumption, not a theorem: it works when the solution changes slowly compared with the perturbation
  • The result is the test equation, written for \(u\) instead of \(\delta\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu \, u , \qquad u(0) = u_0 \tag{6.2} \]

  • Exact solution: \(u(t) = u_0 e^{\mu t}\)

Why \(u\) instead of \(\delta\)

  • Linear tendency \(f(t, u) = \mu u\): the linearized equation (6.1) is the ODE itself
  • A solution and any perturbation obey the same equation: \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = \mu u\) and \(\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}t = \mu\delta\)
  • A numerical method treats both in the same way
  • So the test equation stands for the solution of a linear problem: decay equation, method of lines, oscillator
  • Nonlinear problem: (6.2) describes only the perturbations \(\delta\), with \(\mu\) the frozen derivative \(\partial f/\partial u\)

Sign and meaning of \(\mu\)

  • Decay equation: \(\mu = -k\)
  • Sign: the decay constant \(k > 0\) is a rate of decay, \(\mu = \partial f / \partial u = -k\) is a rate of change
  • \(\mu < 0\): perturbations decay like \(e^{\mu t}\), nearby solutions approach each other (the problem is stable)
  • \(\mu > 0\): perturbations grow like \(e^{\mu t}\), nearby solutions separate
  • The solutions themselves may stay bounded (logistic growth near \(u = 0\))
  • Relaxation \(f = (3 - u)/2\): \(\partial f/\partial u = -\tfrac12\), all solutions approach each other (direction field of lecture 5)

Systems: one test equation per eigenvector

  • System \(\mathrm{d}\vec u/\mathrm{d}t = \vec f(t, \vec u)\) of \(N\) equations
  • Taylor series in \(N\) variables: \(\partial f/\partial u\) becomes the Jacobian \(J\), \(J_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial u_j\)

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec \delta}{\mathrm{d}t} = J \, \vec \delta \tag{6.3} \]

  • \(J\) is evaluated on the solution and frozen
  • Method of lines: \(\vec f = -A\vec u\) is linear, (6.3) holds exactly with \(J = -A\)
  • Assumption: \(N\) linearly independent eigenvectors \(\vec v_m\) with eigenvalues \(\mu_m\)
  • True for symmetric matrices, can fail in general

\[ J \, \vec v_m = \mu_m \, \vec v_m \]

Expanding in the eigenvectors

  • Expand \(\vec \delta(t) = \sum_m c_m(t) \, \vec v_m\) with coordinates \(c_m(t)\)
  • Insert and use \(J \vec v_m = \mu_m \vec v_m\):

\[ \sum_m \frac{\mathrm{d}c_m}{\mathrm{d}t} \, \vec v_m = \sum_m \mu_m \, c_m \, \vec v_m \]

The system falls apart

  • Coordinates in a basis are unique, so the coefficients of each \(\vec v_m\) agree:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}c_m}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mu_m \, c_m , \qquad m = 1, \dots, N \tag{6.4} \]

  • \(N\) test equations (6.2), one per eigenvector, with the eigenvalue \(\mu_m\) as coefficient

Sign convention for the method of lines

  • \(A\) has the positive eigenvalues \(\lambda_m\), the tendency is \(-A\vec u\)
  • \(J = -A\) has the same eigenvectors and the eigenvalues

\[ \mu_m = -\lambda_m = -\frac{4D}{h^2} \sin^2 \frac{\theta_m}{2} < 0 , \qquad m = 1, \dots, N \tag{6.5} \]

  • \(\theta_m = m\pi/(N+1)\)
  • On the negative real axis, between about \(-D\pi^2/((N+1)h)^2\) and \(-4D/h^2\)

Complex coefficients: the oscillator

  • The eigenvalues of a non-symmetric real matrix can be complex: this is how oscillations enter the test equation
  • \(\mathrm{d}^2u/\mathrm{d}t^2 = -\omega^2 u\), angular frequency \(\omega > 0\) (spring, small pendulum)
  • Solutions \(\cos\omega t\), \(\sin\omega t\), period \(2\pi/\omega\)
  • First-order system with the velocity \(v = \mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t\):

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v \\ -\omega^2 u \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}}_{J} \begin{pmatrix} u \\ v \end{pmatrix} \]

Eigenvalues of the oscillator

  • Eigenvalues: roots of \(\det(J - \mu I) = 0\), \(I\) the \(2 \times 2\) identity

\[ \det \begin{pmatrix} -\mu & 1 \\ -\omega^2 & -\mu \end{pmatrix} = (-\mu)(-\mu) - 1 \cdot (-\omega^2) = \mu^2 + \omega^2 = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \mu = \pm i \omega \]

  • Purely imaginary
  • With \(\mu = i\omega\): \(c(t) = c_0 e^{i\omega t}\), a rotation in the complex plane with angular frequency \(\omega\) (lecture 4)

Four rules for complex numbers

  • \(z = a + ib\), real part \(a = \operatorname{Re} z\), imaginary part \(b = \operatorname{Im} z\)
  • These \(a\), \(b\) are not the coefficients \(a_i\), \(b_i\) of the two-stage methods
  1. Modulus \(|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\): distance from the origin, for real \(z\) the absolute value
  2. Complex conjugate \(\bar z = a - ib\)
  3. \(|z_1 z_2| = |z_1|\,|z_2|\) and \(|z_1/z_2| = |z_1|/|z_2|\): with \(z = |z| e^{i\varphi}\) (\(\varphi\) the angle of \(z\)), \(z_1 z_2 = |z_1||z_2| e^{i(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)}\) and \(|e^{i(\varphi_1 + \varphi_2)}| = 1\)
  4. \(|e^z| = e^a\), because \(e^{a + ib} = e^a e^{ib}\) and \(|e^{ib}| = 1\) (Euler’s formula)

The modulus of the exact solution

By rule 4:

\[ |u(t)| = |u_0| \, |e^{\mu t}| = |u_0| \, e^{(\operatorname{Re} \mu) \, t} \tag{6.6} \]

  • Only \(\operatorname{Re}\mu\) decides about growth and decay
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu < 0\): decay
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu = 0\): constant modulus (undamped oscillation)
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu > 0\): growth
  • \(\operatorname{Im}\mu\): rotation in the complex plane, an oscillation of the real state with angular frequency \(\operatorname{Im}\mu\)

Real states from complex eigenvalues

  • The state \(\vec u\) is real, eigenvectors and coordinates in (6.4) may be complex
  • Real \(J\): conjugating \(J \vec v = \mu \vec v\) with \(\bar J = J\) gives \(J \bar{\vec v} = \bar\mu \, \bar{\vec v}\)
  • Complex eigenvalues therefore come in conjugate pairs
  • In a real vector the coordinates of the partners are conjugate, the imaginary parts cancel
  • Oscillator: \(e^{i\omega t}\) and \(e^{-i\omega t}\) combine to the real solutions \(\cos\omega t\), \(\sin\omega t\)

Example: the predator–prey equations

  • Linearize around the equilibrium \((x, y) = (c/\gamma, a/\alpha)\) of unit 5 (systems of equations)
  • Tendency \(\vec f = (ax - \alpha xy, \; -cy + \gamma xy)\):

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial y} \\[2mm] \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a - \alpha y & -\alpha x \\ \gamma y & -c + \gamma x \end{pmatrix} \]

Predator–prey at the equilibrium

  • At the equilibrium \(a - \alpha \cdot a/\alpha = 0\) and \(-c + \gamma \cdot c/\gamma = 0\): the diagonal vanishes

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\alpha c / \gamma \\ \gamma a / \alpha & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \det (J - \mu I) = \mu^2 - \Big( -\frac{\alpha c}{\gamma} \Big) \frac{\gamma a}{\alpha} = \mu^2 + a c = 0 \]

  • \(\mu = \pm i\sqrt{ac}\): purely imaginary, as for the oscillator
  • Small oscillations around the equilibrium have the angular frequency \(\sqrt{ac}\) and neither grow nor decay

Result: the test equation

Result.

  • Perturbations obey (6.3) with the Jacobian \(J\)
  • Frozen \(J\) with \(N\) independent eigenvectors gives \(N\) test equations \(\mathrm{d}c_m/\mathrm{d}t = \mu_m c_m\) (6.4)
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu\) decides growth or decay (6.6)
  • \(\operatorname{Im}\mu\) is an angular frequency
  • Diffusion gives real negative \(\mu_m\) (6.5)
  • Oscillations give imaginary \(\mu\)
  • A method that is correct for the test equation with every \(\mu_m\) is correct for small perturbations

The amplification factor

The exact propagator

Goal: what each method does to the test equation in one step. One number contains order and stability.

  • Exact solution: \(u(t + \Delta t) = u_0 e^{\mu(t + \Delta t)} = e^{\mu\Delta t}\,u(t)\)
  • Every step multiplies by the exact propagator \(e^{\mu\Delta t}\)
  • It depends only on the dimensionless product

\[ z = \mu \, \Delta t \tag{6.7} \]

  • Decay: \(z = -k\Delta t\)
  • Diffusion: \(z_m = -\lambda_m \Delta t\)
  • Oscillator: \(z = \pm i\omega\Delta t\)

The amplification factor

  • Each method of lecture 5 also multiplies by a fixed factor in every step
  • Applying it \(n\) times:

\[ u_{n+1} = R(z) \, u_n \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad u_n = R(z)^n \, u_0 \tag{6.8} \]

  • \(R(z)\): the amplification factor (also: stability function, discrete propagator)
  • New meaning: in unit 5, \(R\) was a Taylor remainder
  • \(R(z)\) follows by inserting \(f(t, u) = \mu u\)

Forward and backward Euler

Forward Euler: \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \Delta t\,\mu u_n = (1 + z)\,u_n\)

\[ R(z) = 1 + z \tag{6.9} \]

  • For \(z = -k\Delta t\): the factor \(1 - k\Delta t\) of lecture 1

Backward Euler: \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \Delta t\,\mu u_{n+1}\)

  • Collect \(u_{n+1}\) on the left: \((1 - z)\,u_{n+1} = u_n\)
  • Divide (\(z \ne 1\)):

\[ R(z) = \frac{1}{1 - z} \tag{6.10} \]

The trapezoidal method

Trapezoidal method: \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \frac{\Delta t}{2}(\mu u_n + \mu u_{n+1})\)

  • Collect: \(\big(1 - \frac{z}{2}\big)u_{n+1} = \big(1 + \frac{z}{2}\big)u_n\)
  • Divide (\(z \ne 2\)):

\[ R(z) = \frac{1 + z/2}{1 - z/2} \tag{6.11} \]

Heun’s method

  • Stage 1: \(s_1 = \mu u_n\)
  • Stage 2: \(s_2 = \mu(u_n + \Delta t\,s_1) = \mu(u_n + z u_n) = \mu(1 + z)\,u_n\)

\[ u_{n+1} = u_n + \frac{\Delta t}{2} \big( \mu u_n + \mu (1 + z) u_n \big) = \Big( 1 + \frac{z}{2} + \frac{z}{2} (1 + z) \Big) u_n \]

\[ R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} \tag{6.12} \]

  • The midpoint method has the same \(R(z)\)
  • Check: \(s_2 = \mu(1 + z/2)\,u_n\) and \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \Delta t\,s_2\)

The classical Runge–Kutta method

  • Each stage is \(\mu\) times the state at which it is evaluated
  • Write each stage as a multiple of \(\mu u_n\), using \(\tfrac12\Delta t\,s_1 = \tfrac{z}{2}u_n\) and so on:

\[ \begin{aligned} s_1 &= \mu u_n , \\ s_2 &= \mu \big( u_n + \tfrac12 \Delta t \, s_1 \big) = \mu \big( 1 + \tfrac{z}{2} \big) u_n , \\ s_3 &= \mu \big( u_n + \tfrac12 \Delta t \, s_2 \big) = \mu \big( 1 + \tfrac{z}{2} + \tfrac{z^2}{4} \big) u_n , \\ s_4 &= \mu \big( u_n + \Delta t \, s_3 \big) = \mu \big( 1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{4} \big) u_n \end{aligned} \]

Weighted sum of the brackets:

\[ 1 + 2\big(1 + \tfrac{z}{2}\big) + 2\big(1 + \tfrac{z}{2} + \tfrac{z^2}{4}\big) + \big(1 + z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \tfrac{z^3}{4}\big) = 6 + 3 z + z^2 + \tfrac{z^3}{4} \]

RK4: the amplification factor

With \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \frac{\Delta t}{6}(s_1 + 2s_2 + 2s_3 + s_4)\) and \(\Delta t\,\mu = z\):

\[ u_{n+1} = \Big( 1 + \frac{z}{6} \big( 6 + 3z + z^2 + \tfrac{z^3}{4} \big) \Big) u_n \]

\[ R(z) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24} \tag{6.13} \]

Amplification factor and order

  • \(e^z = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{6} + \frac{z^4}{24} + \dots\)
  • Forward Euler, Heun, RK4 are its first terms: up to \(z\), \(z^2\), \(z^4\)
  • These are the orders 1, 2, 4: no coincidence
  • Local truncation error: the left-over when the exact solution is inserted into \(u_{n+1} = R(z)\,u_n\)

\[ \tau_n = u(t_{n+1}) - R(z) \, u(t_n) = \big( e^{z} - R(z) \big) \, u(t_n) \tag{6.14} \]

  • Order \(p\) means \(\tau_n = O(\Delta t^{p+1})\)
  • With \(z = \mu\Delta t\) and \(\mu\) fixed: \(e^z - R(z) = O(z^{p+1})\)
  • \(R\) agrees with \(e^z\) up to and including the term \(z^p\)

The implicit methods

  • Geometric series \(\frac{1}{1 - w} = 1 + w + w^2 + \dots\) for \(|w| < 1\), so for small \(|z|\)
  • Backward Euler: \(\frac{1}{1 - z} = 1 + z + z^2 + \dots\)
  • Term \(z^2\): \(1\) instead of \(\tfrac12\), order 1
  • Trapezoidal, \(w = z/2\): \(\frac{1 + z/2}{1 - z/2} = \big(1 + \frac{z}{2}\big)\big(1 + \frac{z}{2} + \frac{z^2}{4} + \frac{z^3}{8} + \dots\big) = 1 + z + \frac{z^2}{2} + \frac{z^3}{4} + \dots\)
  • Term \(z^3\): \(\tfrac14\) instead of \(\tfrac16\), order 2
  • One direction only: order \(p\) implies \(R = e^z + O(z^{p+1})\)
  • Agreement for the test equation proves the order only for linear problems
  • Trapezoidal method: order 2 also for nonlinear problems (quoted)
  • Check: halving \(z\) divides \(|e^z - R(z)|\) by about \(2^{p+1}\), here at \(z = -0.01\)
forward Euler  : order p = 1.00
backward Euler : order p = 0.99
trapezoidal    : order p = 1.99
Heun           : order p = 2.00
RK4            : order p = 4.00

Real coefficients: decay

  • \(z = -k\Delta t < 0\)
  • The numerical solution behaves like the exact one (decay without sign change) only if \(0 \le R(z) < 1\)

Forward Euler, \(R = 1 - k\Delta t\)

  • \(|R| \le 1\) needs \(k\Delta t \le 2\)
  • Sign change in every step for \(k\Delta t > 1\)

Backward Euler, \(R = 1/(1 + k\Delta t)\)

  • The denominator is larger than 1, so \(0 < R < 1\) for every \(k\Delta t > 0\)

The trapezoidal method for decay

Trapezoidal, \(R = (1 - k\Delta t/2)/(1 + k\Delta t/2)\)

  • \(|1 - k\Delta t/2| < 1 + k\Delta t/2\), so \(|R| < 1\) for every \(k\Delta t > 0\)
  • But the numerator is negative for \(k\Delta t > 2\): sign change in every step
  • \(k\Delta t \to \infty\): \(R \to (-\tfrac12)/(\tfrac12) = -1\), an oscillation that hardly decays

Heun and RK4 for decay

Heun, \(R = 1 - k\Delta t + (k\Delta t)^2/2\)

  • Completing the square: \(R = \frac12\big((1 - k\Delta t)^2 + 1\big) \ge \tfrac12\), so no sign change
  • \(R \le 1\) ⇔ \(k\Delta t\,(k\Delta t/2 - 1) \le 0\) ⇔ \(k\Delta t \le 2\), the forward Euler limit

RK4

  • \(|R| \le 1\) for \(k\Delta t \le 2.785\)
  • 2.785 is the nonzero root of \(R(-k\Delta t) = 1\), a cubic after dividing by \(k\Delta t\), solved numerically

The five factors for the decay equation

  • Grey band: \(|R| \le 1\)
  • Small \(k\Delta t\): all follow \(e^{-k\Delta t}\), the more closely the higher the order
  • Large \(k\Delta t\): the explicit methods leave the band
  • Backward Euler goes to 0, the trapezoidal method to \(-1\)

Result: the amplification factor

Result.

  • A one-step method multiplies the solution of the test equation by \(R(z)\) in every step (6.8)
  • The factors of the methods are (6.9)–(6.13)
  • Order \(p\): \(R(z) = e^z + O(z^{p+1})\)
  • Decay equation, explicit methods: \(k\Delta t \le 2\) (Euler, Heun), \(k\Delta t \le 2.785\) (RK4)
  • Backward Euler and trapezoidal: \(|R| < 1\) for every time step
  • Trapezoidal: sign change in every step for \(k\Delta t > 2\)

Stability regions, A- and L-stability

The stability region

Goal: all \(z\) for which the numerical solution does not grow, the largest stable time step, the behaviour for very large \(|z|\).

  • By (6.8) and rule 3: \(|u_n| = |R(z)|^n\,|u_0|\)
  • Bounded for all \(n\) if and only if \(|R(z)| \le 1\)
  • Absolutely stable for \(z\): \(|R(z)| \le 1\)
  • The stability region:

\[ \mathcal S = \{ z \in \mathbb C : |R(z)| \le 1 \} \tag{6.15} \]

  • \(|R| = 1\): the modulus neither grows nor decays, important for oscillations (lecture 7)
  • The exact solution does not grow for \(\operatorname{Re} z \le 0\) (6.6), since \(\operatorname{Re} z = \Delta t\,\operatorname{Re}\mu\) and \(\Delta t > 0\)
  • Ideal stability region: the left half-plane
  • Below: \(z = a + ib\)

Forward Euler

  • \(|1 + z| \le 1\): the distance of \(z\) from \(-1\) is at most 1

\[ |1 + z|^2 = (1 + a)^2 + b^2 \le 1 \]

  • Stability region: the disk of radius 1 around \(-1\)
  • Real axis: \(-2 \le z \le 0\)
  • Imaginary axis: \(|1 + ib|^2 = 1 + b^2 > 1\) for every \(b \ne 0\)
  • Every oscillation is amplified by \(\sqrt{1 + b^2}\) per step
  • This is the outward spiral of predator–prey in lecture 5

Backward Euler

  • By rule 3: \(|R| = 1/|1 - z| \le 1\) ⇔ \(|1 - z| \ge 1\), the distance from \(+1\) is at least 1
  • Stability region: everything outside the disk of radius 1 around \(+1\)
  • It contains the left half-plane: for \(a \le 0\), \(|1 - z|^2 = (1 - a)^2 + b^2 \ge (1 - a)^2 \ge 1\)

Trapezoidal method: stability region

  • \(|R| \le 1\) ⇔ \(|1 + z/2| \le |1 - z/2|\)
  • Multiply by 2 and square (both sides \(\ge 0\)):

\[ |2 + z|^2 \le |2 - z|^2 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad (2 + a)^2 + b^2 \le (2 - a)^2 + b^2 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 8 a \le 0 \]

  • Last step: \(4 + 4a + a^2 \le 4 - 4a + a^2\)
  • Stability region: exactly the left half-plane, as for the exact solution
  • Imaginary axis: \(|R| = 1\), oscillations are neither amplified nor damped

Heun on the imaginary axis

  • Real axis: \(-2 \le z \le 0\)
  • Imaginary axis: \(z = ib\), \(z^2 = -b^2\), \(R(ib) = \big(1 - \frac{b^2}{2}\big) + ib\)

\[ |R(i b)|^2 = \Big( 1 - \frac{b^2}{2} \Big)^2 + b^2 = 1 - b^2 + \frac{b^4}{4} + b^2 = 1 + \frac{b^4}{4} > 1 \quad \text{for } b \ne 0 \]

  • Heun amplifies every oscillation, like forward Euler
  • But only by \(1 + O(b^4)\) instead of \(1 + O(b^2)\)

RK4 on the imaginary axis

  • \(z^2 = -b^2\), \(z^3 = -ib^3\), \(z^4 = b^4\)
  • \(R(ib) = \big(1 - \frac{b^2}{2} + \frac{b^4}{24}\big) + i\big(b - \frac{b^3}{6}\big)\)
  • Square and add real and imaginary part, term by term:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Big(1 - \frac{b^2}{2} + \frac{b^4}{24}\Big)^2 &= 1 - b^2 + \frac{b^4}{4} + \frac{b^4}{12} - \frac{b^6}{24} + \frac{b^8}{576} , \\ \Big(b - \frac{b^3}{6}\Big)^2 &= b^2 - \frac{b^4}{3} + \frac{b^6}{36} , \\ |R(ib)|^2 &= 1 + 0 \cdot b^2 + 0 \cdot b^4 - \frac{b^6}{72} + \frac{b^8}{576} \end{aligned} \]

RK4 on the imaginary axis: result

  • The \(b^4\) terms cancel: \(\frac14 + \frac{1}{12} = \frac13\)
  • The \(b^6\) terms: \(-\frac{1}{24} + \frac{1}{36} = -\frac{1}{72}\)
  • \(|R(ib)|^2 \le 1\) ⇔ \(b^6\big(\frac{b^2}{576} - \frac{1}{72}\big) \le 0\) ⇔ \(b^2 \le 8\)
  • RK4 is stable for \(|b| \le 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.83\) and damps oscillations slightly there

The stability regions

  • Explicit regions are bounded and grow with the order
  • Only RK4 contains a piece of the imaginary axis
  • The implicit regions contain the whole left half-plane

The largest stable time step

  • Forward Euler on \(\mathrm{d}\vec u/\mathrm{d}t = J\vec u\) with \(\vec u_n = \sum_m c_{m,n}\vec v_m\)
  • \(c_{m,n}\): coordinate of eigenvector \(m\) at time level \(n\)

\[ \vec u_{n+1} = \vec u_n + \Delta t \, J \vec u_n = \sum_m c_{m,n} \big( \vec v_m + \Delta t \, \mu_m \vec v_m \big) = \sum_m (1 + \mu_m \Delta t) \, c_{m,n} \, \vec v_m \]

  • Comparing coefficients: \(c_{m,n+1} = R(\mu_m\Delta t)\,c_{m,n}\)
  • Backward Euler: \((I - \Delta t J)\vec u_{n+1} = \vec u_n\) gives \((1 - \mu_m\Delta t)\,c_{m,n+1} = c_{m,n}\)
  • The same for every method: each component is multiplied by \(R\) of its own \(z_m = \mu_m\Delta t\)

Stability condition for a system

Stability condition for a system. \(\Delta t\) must be chosen such that \(z_m = \mu_m\Delta t\) lies in \(\mathcal S\) for every eigenvalue \(\mu_m\) of \(J\).

  • The \(z_m\) lie on rays from the origin through the \(\mu_m\)
  • Increasing \(\Delta t\) moves them outward
  • The largest stable \(\Delta t\) is reached when the first \(z_m\) leaves \(\mathcal S\)

Diffusion

  • \(z_m = -\lambda_m\Delta t\) lie on the negative real axis (6.5)
  • Forward Euler and Heun: \(\lambda_N\Delta t \le 2\)
  • RK4: \(\lambda_N\Delta t \le 2.785\)
  • \(\lambda_N < 4D/h^2\) (because \(\sin^2(\theta_N/2) < 1\)), so sufficient for forward Euler:

\[ \Delta t \le \frac{2}{4 D / h^2} = \frac{h^2}{2 D} \tag{6.16} \]

  • As found in lecture 5
  • Halving \(h\) divides the largest stable \(\Delta t\) by four

Cost of explicit methods for diffusion

  • RK4 allows a step \(2.785/2 \approx 1.4\) times larger, but needs four evaluations of the tendency per step
  • Per evaluation, RK4 advances by at most \(2.785/(4\lambda_N) \approx 0.70/\lambda_N\)
  • Forward Euler advances by \(2/\lambda_N\), almost three times as far
  • When stability dictates \(\Delta t\) (diffusion, fine grid), the higher order is not cheaper

An example with complex eigenvalues

  • Linear system \(\mathrm{d}\vec u/\mathrm{d}t = J\vec u\), \(J\) not symmetric:

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix} -21 & 19 & -20 \\ 19 & -21 & 20 \\ 40 & -40 & -40 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \vec u(0) = (1, 0, -1)^T \]

eigenvalues: [ -2. +0.j -40.+40.j -40.-40.j]
  • By hand: \(J(1, 1, 0)^T = (-2, -2, 0)^T\), so \(\mu_1 = -2\)
  • The other two: the conjugate pair \(\mu_{2,3} = -40 \pm 40i\)
  • All \(\operatorname{Re}\mu < 0\): all solutions decay
  • The component of \(\mu_1\) decays on the time scale \(1/2\)
  • The oscillating pair decays on the much shorter time scale \(1/40\)

The time-step limit of the example

  • Forward Euler: each \(z_m\) must lie in the disk \(|1 + z| \le 1\)
  • \(\mu_1 = -2\): \(2\Delta t \le 2\), that is \(\Delta t \le 1\)
  • \(\mu_2 = -40 + 40i\): \(1 + z_2 = (1 - 40\Delta t) + 40\Delta t\,i\)

\[ \begin{aligned} |1 + z_2|^2 &= (1 - 40 \Delta t)^2 + (40 \Delta t)^2 = 1 - 80 \Delta t + 3200 \Delta t^2 \le 1 \\ &\Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta t \, (3200 \Delta t - 80) \le 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Delta t \le \frac{1}{40} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(\mu_3 = \bar\mu_2\) gives the same condition, because \(|1 + \bar z| = |1 + z|\)
  • The fast pair decides: \(\Delta t \le 0.025\), although the slow component allows 40 times more

Check with forward Euler

  • Forward Euler to \(t = 2\), just below and just above the limit:
dt = 0.024: largest |u_i| at t = 2 is 0.0313
dt = 0.026: largest |u_i| at t = 2 is 17.4
  • Exact at \(t = 2\): dominated by the slow component, decayed by \(e^{-4} \approx 0.018\)

A-stability

  • Eigenvalues far out in the left half-plane: explicit methods need very small time steps
  • A-stable: the stability region contains the whole left half-plane

\[ \{ z : \operatorname{Re} z \le 0 \} \subseteq \mathcal S \]

  • An A-stable method is stable for every \(\Delta t\) whenever the exact solution does not grow
  • Backward Euler and the trapezoidal method are A-stable

No explicit method is A-stable

  • Each stage of an explicit method is \(\mu\) times (\(u_n\) + \(\Delta t\) × earlier stages)
  • So each stage is \(\mu u_n\) times a polynomial in \(z\), and \(R(z)\) is a polynomial
  • A consistent method has \(R(z) = 1 + z + \dots\), a polynomial of degree \(d \ge 1\)
  • For large \(|z|\) the highest term dominates: \(|R| \to \infty\) for \(z \to -\infty\)
  • The stability region of an explicit method is bounded
  • Only implicit methods (\(R\) a quotient of polynomials) can be A-stable

Too much of a good thing

  • Backward Euler is stable far into the right half-plane, where the exact solution grows
  • \(z = 3\): \(R = 1/(1 - 3) = -\tfrac12\), while \(e^3 \approx 20\)
  • A physical instability can be damped away by the method

L-stability

  • The two A-stable methods for large \(|z|\), dividing numerator and denominator by \(z\):

\[ \text{backward Euler: } R(z) = \frac{1}{1 - z} \to 0 , \qquad \text{trapezoidal: } R(z) = \frac{1/z + 1/2}{1/z - 1/2} \to \frac{1/2}{-1/2} = -1 , \qquad \text{for } |z| \to \infty \]

  • Exact: \(e^z \to 0\) for \(\operatorname{Re} z \to -\infty\), a very fast component is gone after one step
  • Backward Euler reproduces this
  • The trapezoidal method keeps such a component with nearly unchanged amplitude and flips its sign in every step
  • L-stable: A-stable and \(R(z) \to 0\) for \(|z| \to \infty\)
  • Backward Euler is L-stable, the trapezoidal method is not

When L-stability matters

  • Method of lines: \(z_N = -\lambda_N\Delta t \approx -4D\Delta t/h^2\)
  • With \(\Delta t\) chosen for the accuracy of the long waves, \(|z_N|\) can be in the hundreds
  • Short waves at a sharp front: the trapezoidal method keeps them as a zigzag
  • The trapezoidal method for diffusion is the Crank–Nicolson method (lecture 12)

Zigzag: trapezoidal vs. backward Euler

  • \(D = 1\), \(N = 49\), five steps with \(\Delta t = 0.01\) (\(\lambda_N\Delta t \approx 100\))
  • Backward Euler damps the short waves, but, of first order, smooths the long waves too much
  • In practice: L-stable methods of higher order (BDF, lecture 7, and implicit Runge–Kutta methods)

Result: stability regions

Result.

  • Stability region (6.15): \(|R(z)| \le 1\)
  • Forward Euler: the disk of radius 1 around \(-1\)
  • Backward Euler: everything outside the disk of radius 1 around \(+1\)
  • Trapezoidal: exactly the left half-plane
  • Explicit methods have bounded regions, only RK4 contains a piece of the imaginary axis, \(|\operatorname{Im} z| \le 2\sqrt2\)
  • Every \(z_m = \mu_m\Delta t\) of a system must lie in the region, for diffusion \(\Delta t \le h^2/(2D)\) (6.16) with forward Euler

Result: A- and L-stability

Result.

  • A-stable (backward Euler, trapezoidal): the whole left half-plane
  • No explicit method is A-stable
  • L-stable (backward Euler): in addition \(R \to 0\) for \(|z| \to \infty\)
  • Trapezoidal: \(R \to -1\), fast components remain as a zigzag

Consistency, stability and convergence

Consistency

Goal: make consistency, stability, convergence of unit 1 precise for one-step methods, show that consistency and stability give convergence, state the Lax equivalence theorem.

  • One-step methods: \(u_{n+1} = u_n + \Delta t\,\Phi(t_n, u_n, \Delta t)\) with the increment function \(\Phi\), on \(t_0 \le t \le T\)
  • Lecture 5: forward Euler has \(\Phi = f\), Runge–Kutta methods a weighted mean of values of \(f\)
  • Consistent: the local truncation error \(\tau_n\) satisfies

\[ \max_n \frac{|\tau_n|}{\Delta t} \to 0 \qquad \text{for } \Delta t \to 0 \]

  • The maximum is over all steps in \([t_0, T]\)

Consistency and order

  • Order \(p \ge 1\) implies consistency: \(|\tau_n| \le C\Delta t^{p+1}\) gives \(|\tau_n|/\Delta t \le C\Delta t^p \to 0\)
  • Dividing by \(\Delta t\) is essential: \(\tau_n/\Delta t\) is the error per unit time
  • It is what remains when the formula of the method, divided by \(\Delta t\), is compared with the ODE

A simple test for consistency

  • Assumptions: \(\Phi\) continuously differentiable in \(\Delta t\), exact solution twice continuously differentiable
  • Taylor: \(u(t_{n+1}) = u(t_n) + \Delta t\,f\big(t_n, u(t_n)\big) + O(\Delta t^2)\)
  • Taylor in the third argument: \(\Phi\big(t_n, u(t_n), \Delta t\big) = \Phi\big(t_n, u(t_n), 0\big) + O(\Delta t)\)

\[ \frac{\tau_n}{\Delta t} = \frac{u(t_{n+1}) - u(t_n) - \Delta t \, \Phi\big(t_n, u(t_n), \Delta t\big)}{\Delta t} = f\big(t_n, u(t_n)\big) - \Phi\big(t_n, u(t_n), 0\big) + O(\Delta t) \]

  • This goes to zero if and only if

\[ \Phi(t, u, 0) = f(t, u) \tag{6.17} \]

  • For a vanishing time step the increment must be the tendency

Consistency of Runge–Kutta methods

  • Runge–Kutta: at \(\Delta t = 0\) all stages equal \(f(t_n, u_n)\), so \(\Phi(t, u, 0) = \sum_i b_i\,f(t, u)\)
  • (6.17) is the condition \(\sum_i b_i = 1\), the first order condition

Stability

  • Stable: there are constants \(C_s\) and \(\Delta t_0 > 0\) such that any two numerical solutions \(u_n\), \(\tilde u_n\) satisfy

\[ |u_n - \tilde u_n| \le C_s \, |u_0 - \tilde u_0| \qquad \text{for all } n \text{ with } t_n \le T \tag{6.18} \]

  • Both computed with the same \(\Delta t \le \Delta t_0\), from the initial values \(u_0\) and \(\tilde u_0\)
  • \(C_s\) does not depend on \(\Delta t\)
  • An initial error grows at most by a fixed factor, however many steps are needed to reach \(T\)
  • Also called zero-stability, because it concerns the limit \(\Delta t \to 0\)

Convergence, and stability of one-step methods

  • Convergent: the global error \(E_n = u(t_n) - u_n\), starting from the exact initial value, satisfies

\[ \max_n |E_n| \to 0 \qquad \text{for } \Delta t \to 0 \]

  • The maximum is over all \(n\) with \(t_n \le T\)

One-step methods with a Lipschitz increment function are stable (constant \(L_\Phi\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} |u_{n+1} - \tilde u_{n+1}| &= \big| u_n - \tilde u_n + \Delta t \big( \Phi(t_n, u_n, \Delta t) - \Phi(t_n, \tilde u_n, \Delta t) \big) \big| \\ &\le (1 + L_\Phi \Delta t) \, |u_n - \tilde u_n| \end{aligned} \]

  • Apply \(n\) times and use \(1 + x \le e^x\): \(|u_n - \tilde u_n| \le e^{L_\Phi(t_n - t_0)}|u_0 - \tilde u_0|\)
  • So \(C_s = e^{L_\Phi(T - t_0)}\), independent of \(\Delta t\)

Consistency and stability give convergence

  • Lecture 5: order \(p\) and a Lipschitz increment function give convergence
  • Two ingredients: a bound for the local error (consistency) and a bound for the growth of earlier errors (stability)
  • Clearest for the test equation

Step 1: the error recursion

  • Numerical: \(u_{n+1} = R\,u_n\) with \(R = R(\mu\Delta t)\)
  • Exact, by (6.14): \(u(t_{n+1}) = R\,u(t_n) + \tau_n\)
  • Subtract:

\[ E_{n+1} = R \, E_n + \tau_n \tag{6.19} \]

  • The method propagates the existing error with \(R\) and adds the new local error

Unrolling the recursion

Step 2

  • \(E_0 = 0\), \(E_1 = \tau_0\), \(E_2 = R\tau_0 + \tau_1\), \(E_3 = R^2\tau_0 + R\tau_1 + \tau_2\), in general

\[ E_n = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} R^{\,n-1-j} \, \tau_j \]

  • The local error of step \(j\) is propagated by the remaining \(n - 1 - j\) steps

Step 3: the two bounds

  • Consistency: \(|\tau_j| \le C\Delta t^{p+1}\)
  • Stability: \(|R|^i \le C_s\) for all \(i \le n\)
  • With rule 3, the triangle inequality and \(n\Delta t = t_n - t_0\):

\[ |E_n| \le \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} C_s \, C \Delta t^{p+1} = n \, C_s \, C \Delta t^{p+1} = C_s \, C \, (t_n - t_0) \, \Delta t^p \tag{6.20} \]

  • The global error goes to zero like \(\Delta t^p\): convergence of order \(p\)

Why the powers of \(R\) are bounded

  • Consistent: \(R(z) = 1 + z + O(z^2)\)
  • So there is a constant \(\kappa\) with \(|R(z) - 1 - z| \le \kappa|z|^2\) for small \(|z|\)
  • Triangle inequality with \(z = \mu\Delta t\) and \(\Delta t \le \Delta t_0\):

\[ |R(\mu \Delta t)| \le 1 + |\mu| \Delta t + \kappa \, |\mu|^2 \Delta t^2 \le 1 + \underbrace{\big( |\mu| + \kappa \, |\mu|^2 \Delta t_0 \big)}_{\beta} \, \Delta t \]

  • \(\beta\) depends on \(\mu\) and \(\Delta t_0\), not on \(\Delta t\)
  • Then \(|R|^i \le (1 + \beta\Delta t)^i \le e^{\beta\,i\Delta t} \le e^{\beta(T - t_0)} = C_s\)
  • One-step methods: stability (6.18) is automatic, consistency alone gives convergence
  • Multistep methods (several earlier time levels): not so, a consistent multistep method can diverge (lecture 7)

Two meanings of stability

Stability (6.18), zero-stability Absolute stability (6.15)
Question Errors bounded as \(\Delta t \to 0\) on fixed \([t_0, T]\)? Solution bounded as \(n \to \infty\) for fixed \(\Delta t\)?
Needed for convergence a usable result with the chosen time step
One-step methods always, if consistent only if \(z = \mu\Delta t \in \mathcal S\) for all \(\mu\)

Forward Euler, decay equation with \(k = 1000\) on \(0 \le t \le 1\)

  • It converges: the error goes to zero like \(\Delta t\)
  • But with \(\Delta t = 0.01\): \(R = 1 - 10 = -9\)
  • After 100 steps a round-off error of \(10^{-16}\) is multiplied by \(9^{100} \approx 10^{95}\)
  • Convergence is a statement about the limit
  • Absolute stability decides whether a particular \(\Delta t\) is usable
  • We need both

The Lax equivalence theorem

  • Linear PDE, grid spacing \(h\), one-step method in time: \(\vec u_{n+1} = B\,\vec u_n\)
  • \(B\) depends on \(h\) and \(\Delta t\), for forward Euler on the method of lines \(B = I - \Delta t A\)
  • Space and time are refined together, for example with \(\Delta t/h^2\) fixed
  • Stability: \(\|B^n\| \le C_s\) for all \(n\) with \(t_n \le T\), uniformly as \(h, \Delta t \to 0\)

Lax equivalence theorem (Lax and Richtmyer, 1956). For a consistent discretization of a well-posed linear initial value problem, stability is necessary and sufficient for convergence.

  • Quoted without proof (LeVeque 2007)
  • Sufficient: the argument of (6.20) with matrices in place of numbers
  • Necessary: an unstable scheme cannot converge, however consistent it is
  • Convergence needs the unknown exact solution
  • The theorem replaces it by two checks of the scheme alone: consistency (Taylor series) and stability (this lecture)

Example: forward Euler for diffusion

  • Fix \(r = D\Delta t/h^2\), refine \(h\) and \(\Delta t\) together
  • \(B = I - \Delta t A\) is symmetric with the eigenvalues \(1 - \lambda_m\Delta t\)
  • \(B\vec v_m = (1 - \lambda_m\Delta t)\vec v_m\) applied \(n\) times: \(B^n\vec v_m = (1 - \lambda_m\Delta t)^n\vec v_m\)
  • 2-norm of a symmetric matrix (lecture 4):

\[ \|B^n\|_2 = \max_m |1 - \lambda_m \Delta t|^n , \qquad \lambda_m \Delta t = 4 r \sin^2 \frac{\theta_m}{2} \]

\(r \le \tfrac12\)

  • \(0 < \lambda_m\Delta t < 4r \le 2\), so every \(|1 - \lambda_m\Delta t| \le 1\) and \(\|B^n\|_2 \le 1\)
  • Stable, therefore convergent

Forward Euler for diffusion: \(r > \tfrac12\)

  • On fine grids \(\lambda_N\Delta t \to 4r > 2\), since \(\sin^2(\theta_N/2) \to 1\)
  • Then \(|1 - \lambda_N\Delta t| \to 4r - 1 > 1\)
  • Reaching \(T\) takes \(n = (T - t_0)/\Delta t \to \infty\) steps, so \(\|B^n\|_2 \to \infty\)
  • Unstable, does not converge: round-off in the shortest wave grows until it dominates
  • The limit (6.16) is not only a practical restriction, it is the condition for convergence

Result: consistency, stability, convergence

Result.

  • Consistent: \(\tau_n/\Delta t \to 0\), for one-step methods \(\Phi(t, u, 0) = f(t, u)\) (6.17)
  • Stable (zero-stable): initial errors are amplified by at most a fixed factor (6.18)
  • Convergent: the global error goes to zero
  • Consistency of order \(p\) and stability give convergence of order \(p\) (6.20)
  • One-step methods: stability follows from consistency
  • Linear PDEs, Lax equivalence theorem: a consistent scheme converges if and only if it is stable
  • Absolute stability decides whether a fixed \(\Delta t\) is usable

Stiff problems

Think first

Goal: define stiffness, recognize it from the eigenvalues of the Jacobian, decide when an implicit method is worth its cost.

A solution has a fast component, decayed after a short time, and a slow one. After the fast one has gone, only the slow one needs to be resolved. Does forward Euler know that?

A stiff example

  • Relaxation towards a slowly varying value, \(k > 0\) large:

\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\mathrm{d}t} = -k \, \big( u - \cos t \big) , \qquad u(0) = 0 \tag{6.21} \]

  • The relaxation of lecture 5 with \(3 \to \cos t\) and \(\tfrac12 \to k\)
  • Example: a thin body in surroundings with a daily cycle
  • \(\partial f/\partial u = -k\)

The exact solution

  • Linear: \(u' + ku = k\cos t\), solution = particular + homogeneous solution
  • Try \(u_p = \alpha_1\cos t + \alpha_2\sin t\) (new constants, not the \(\alpha\) of predator–prey)
  • Insert: \(u_p' + k u_p = (-\alpha_1 + k\alpha_2)\sin t + (\alpha_2 + k\alpha_1)\cos t = k\cos t\)
  • The coefficients of \(\sin t\) and \(\cos t\) agree on both sides:

\[ -\alpha_1 + k \alpha_2 = 0 , \qquad \alpha_2 + k \alpha_1 = k \]

The exact solution: coefficients

  • First equation: \(\alpha_1 = k\alpha_2\), inserted into the second: \(\alpha_2(1 + k^2) = k\)
  • \(\alpha_2 = k/(1 + k^2)\), \(\alpha_1 = k^2/(1 + k^2)\)
  • Homogeneous solution: \(Ce^{-kt}\), and \(u(0) = 0\) gives \(\alpha_1 + C = 0\)

\[ u(t) = \underbrace{\frac{k^2}{1 + k^2} \cos t + \frac{k}{1 + k^2} \sin t}_{\text{slow: time scale } 1} \; \underbrace{- \frac{k^2}{1 + k^2} \, e^{-kt}}_{\text{fast: time scale } 1/k} \tag{6.22} \]

What the time step must resolve

  • The transient decays on the time scale \(1/k\)
  • After a few multiples of \(1/k\) it is negligible
  • The slow part follows \(\cos t\) with a small lag
  • Accuracy: \(\Delta t \ll 1/k\) during the transient
  • After the transient, \(\Delta t \approx 0.1\) would be accurate enough
  • Stability: forward Euler needs \(k\Delta t \le 2\) throughout, also long after the transient
  • Every step makes a local error, which is a perturbation
  • Perturbations obey \(\mathrm{d}\delta/\mathrm{d}t = -k\delta\)
  • Forward Euler multiplies them by \(1 - k\Delta t\) in every step
  • For \(k\Delta t > 2\) they grow, however small they are
  • The fast time scale is still in the problem, even when it is no longer visible in the solution

The example with \(k = 50\)

What the example shows

  • The first forward Euler step cannot follow the transient: an error of order 1
  • \(k\Delta t = 1.9\): \(R = -0.9\), the error changes sign and decays
  • After about 1.5 time units the solution follows the slow part
  • \(k\Delta t = 2.1\): \(R = -1.1\), the error grows by 10 % per step
  • After the 71 steps to \(t = 3\) the error is about 900
  • A change of \(\Delta t\) by 10 % separates a usable from a useless result
  • Backward Euler with a step five times the forward Euler limit also misses the transient
  • But its factor \(R = 1/(1 + k\Delta t) = 1/11\) removes the error within a few steps

What makes a problem stiff

Stiffness. A problem is stiff if, for an explicit method, the time step is limited by stability rather than by the accuracy required for the solution we are interested in.

  • Stiffness is not a property of the equation alone
  • It also depends on the time interval and the accuracy we want
  • Only the transient of (6.21) is of interest: the problem is not stiff

The stiffness ratio

  • Recognizing stiffness: eigenvalues of \(J\) of very different size in the left half-plane
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu_m\) near zero: slowly decaying components, they make up the solution
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu_m\) far out: fast components, they set the stability limit
  • The stiffness ratio, both extremes over the eigenvalues with \(\operatorname{Re}\mu_m < 0\):

\[ S = \frac{\max_m |\operatorname{Re} \mu_m|}{\min_m |\operatorname{Re} \mu_m|} \tag{6.23} \]

Using the stiffness ratio

  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu_m = 0\) (undamped oscillations): treated separately in lecture 7
  • \(S\) of order 1: explicit methods work well
  • \(S \gg 1\): stiff, an explicit method needs about \(S\) times more steps than the slowest component requires
  • Example (6.21): one eigenvalue \(-k\), the slow time scale 1 comes from the forcing \(\cos t\)
  • The ratio of the two time scales, \(k = 50\), plays the role of \(S\)

Diffusion is stiff

  • For the method of lines (6.5), \(S\) is the condition number of \(A\):

\[ S = \frac{\lambda_N}{\lambda_1} \approx \frac{4D/h^2}{D \pi^2/((N+1)h)^2} = \frac{4 (N+1)^2}{\pi^2} \]

  • \(N = 100\): \(S \approx 4000\)
  • Doubling the resolution multiplies \(S\) by four
  • Every model that resolves diffusion on a fine grid is stiff
  • Vertical diffusion in atmosphere models and groundwater flow are integrated with implicit methods
  • The \(3 \times 3\) example: \(S = 40/2 = 20\), mildly stiff

Nonlinear problems

  • \(J\) is the Jacobian along the solution
  • Its eigenvalues, and with them the stiffness, change during the integration
  • Atmospheric chemistry: some species react within microseconds, others over days

Implicit methods for stiff problems

  • Cost per step: a linear system with \(I - \Delta t J\) (backward Euler) or \(I - \frac{\Delta t}{2}J\) (trapezoidal)
  • Nonlinear problems: a Newton iteration
  • Method of lines: \(J = -A\) is tridiagonal, solved in \(\propto N\) operations (lecture 10), like one evaluation of the tendency
  • In 2D and 3D the linear systems dominate the cost (lecture 11)
  • Worth it if the step chosen by accuracy is so much larger than the explicit stability limit that the extra cost is repaid
  • For \(S\) in the thousands this is almost always the case

Library methods for stiff problems

  • solve_ivp offers implicit methods for stiff problems
  • "BDF": a multistep method of variable order (lecture 7)
  • "Radau": an implicit Runge–Kutta method of order 5, L-stable
  • Both choose \(\Delta t\) by accuracy
  • Test: diffusion, \(N = 99\), \(D = 1\), \(u(x, 0) = \sin\pi x\) (only the longest wave), \(0 \le t \le 0.1\)
RK45  :  1184 steps,  8306 evaluations of the tendency
Radau :    12 steps,    92 evaluations of the tendency
BDF   :    27 steps,    56 evaluations of the tendency

What the comparison shows

  • RK45 needs more than a thousand steps for a solution that decays smoothly by \(e^{-\pi^2 \cdot 0.1} \approx 0.37\)
  • Average step \(0.1/1184 \approx 8.4 \times 10^{-5}\), so \(\lambda_N\Delta t \approx (4 \cdot 10^4) \cdot 8.4 \times 10^{-5} \approx 3.4\)
  • The step controller has found the edge of the RK45 stability region
  • The implicit methods need a few dozen steps, chosen by accuracy alone
  • All three meet the tolerance (time errors below \(10^{-6}\))
  • The difference is only in the work

Which implicit method?

  • Backward Euler: L-stable, but only of first order
  • Trapezoidal: second order and A-stable, but zigzag
  • Production codes: L-stable methods of higher order (BDF, implicit Runge–Kutta)
  • Atmosphere and ocean models: only the stiff terms implicit, the rest explicit
  • These are semi-implicit or implicit–explicit methods (lecture 18)

Result: stiff problems

Result.

  • Stiff: the time step of an explicit method is limited by stability, not by accuracy
  • Recognized by very different negative real parts of the eigenvalues of \(J\), the stiffness ratio (6.23)
  • Fast components that have decayed still limit \(\Delta t\), because every local error excites them again
  • Diffusion on a fine grid is stiff, \(S \approx 4(N+1)^2/\pi^2\)
  • Implicit, preferably L-stable, methods remove the stability limit
  • Their higher cost per step is repaid by time steps chosen for accuracy alone

Summary

  • Test equation \(\mathrm{d}u/\mathrm{d}t = \mu u\) (6.2), \(\mu\) the eigenvalues of the Jacobian
  • \(\operatorname{Re}\mu\) decides growth and decay, \(\operatorname{Im}\mu\) is an angular frequency
  • Amplification factor: \(u_{n+1} = R(z)u_n\), \(z = \mu\Delta t\) (6.8)
  • \(R(z)\): \(1 + z\), \(1/(1 - z)\), \((1 + z/2)/(1 - z/2)\), \(1 + z + z^2/2\), and the Taylor series of \(e^z\) up to \(z^4\)
  • Order \(p\): \(R = e^z + O(z^{p+1})\)

Summary (continued)

  • Stability region \(|R(z)| \le 1\) (6.15): every \(z_m\) must lie in it
  • Explicit methods have bounded regions, for diffusion \(\Delta t \le h^2/(2D)\) (6.16)
  • A-stable: the whole left half-plane (backward Euler, trapezoidal), no explicit method
  • L-stable: also \(R \to 0\) (backward Euler)
  • Consistency + stability = convergence (6.20)
  • One-step methods: consistency implies stability
  • Lax: a consistent scheme converges if and only if it is stable
  • Stiff: decayed fast components limit explicit time steps, diffusion is stiff, implicit L-stable methods are cheaper overall
